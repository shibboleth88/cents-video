# The problem you are looking for: order asserting causation

Putting one fact after another proposes that the first explains the second. No
sentence has to say so. The narration can be entirely true line by line, every
fact correct, and the **sequence** still makes a claim that nothing in it states.

The near-miss to watch for: **a property of a thing is not a reason for choosing
it.** These are close enough that confusing them is reasonable, which is exactly
why order alone converts one into the other.

Invented example of the shape:

```
1  The Hasselblad is the camera that went to the Moon.
2  This is the camera Vivian Keller worked with for thirty years.
```

Both lines are true. Together they say she chose it *because* of the Moon, which
neither line claims and which may be false. Moving line 1 after an account of how
she actually came to the camera removes the claim while keeping both facts.

Openings are where this bites hardest, because the first fact in a film is read
as the reason for everything after it. But it can occur at any adjacency.

## What to ask of the draft

Go through it adjacency by adjacency. For each pair of consecutive lines, ask:
**what causal or explanatory claim does this order create?** Then ask whether
anything in the draft actually asserts that claim. Where the order asserts a
motive, an origin or a cause that no line states, that is an instance.

Do not report an adjacency merely because it is abrupt, or because the two lines
are about different things. The test is whether the order manufactures a
*because* that the script never earns.
