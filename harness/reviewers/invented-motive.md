# The problem you are looking for: order attributing a motive

Putting one fact after another proposes that the first explains the second. No
sentence has to say so. The narration can be entirely true line by line and the
**sequence** still makes a claim nothing in it states.

This reviewer is scoped narrowly and deliberately, to one case: **the order
supplying a reason for a person's decision.** The near-miss is
**a property of a thing is not a reason for choosing it** — close enough that
confusing them is reasonable, which is why sequence alone converts one into the
other. General causation between two facts of the world is *not* in scope; a
previous version of this reviewer covered it and produced readings that could be
neither confirmed nor refuted, which is useless as a gate.

## The test, which has a definite answer

An instance requires all three:

1. **A decision expression.** A line attributes a deliberate act to a person:
   *chose, selected, began, initiated, turned to, set out, decided, removed,
   made, took up.* Weather and markets do not decide; people do. No decision
   expression in the window, no instance.
2. **A property stated immediately before it** — within the two preceding lines
   — that could be read as the reason for that act. A property is a fact about
   the object, the world or the work, not an act.
3. **The draft does not state the actual reason in a way the order is consistent
   with.** This is the decisive clause. Check the whole draft for a stated
   reason, motive or origin for that decision. Then:
   - The draft states a reason, and it comes **before** the property → **not an
     instance.** The order is consistent with the stated motive.
   - The draft states a reason and the order proposes a **different** one →
     **instance.**
   - The draft states **no** reason anywhere, and the order supplies one →
     **instance.** This is the common case, and the one worth catching: an
     invented motive is hardest to see when nothing competes with it.

Order matters in a way that is easy to get backwards. A line that *contains*
both — *"The camera he chose was the most common on Earth"* — is one sentence
making one claim, not an order asserting anything. The shape is **property
line, then decision line**, not the reverse and not both in one.

## Invented examples, worked

```
1  The Hasselblad is the camera that went to the Moon.
2  This is the camera Vivian Keller chose.
```
Decision expression: *chose* (2). Property immediately before: (1). Stated
reason anywhere in the draft: none. → **Instance.** The order says she chose it
because of the Moon.

```
1  Keller needed a square negative and a lens she could afford.
2  The Hasselblad is the camera that went to the Moon.
3  This is the camera she chose.
```
Decision: *chose* (3). Property before: (2). But (1) states the actual reason and
precedes it. → **Not an instance.**

```
1  The tide had taken four houses by 1961.
2  The cliff path closed the following spring.
```
No decision expression. → **Not an instance**, however strongly the order
suggests cause. Out of scope by design.

## What to ask of the draft

Find every line containing a decision expression. For each, look at the two
lines before it and ask whether they state a property that reads as the reason.
Then search the whole draft for a stated reason and apply clause 3.

In your `why`, say explicitly which line holds the decision expression, which
holds the property, and what the draft states as the actual reason — or that it
states none. A report that does not answer clause 3 cannot be checked.
