# The problem you are looking for: a conclusion whose premise is absent

Scripts get cut for length. When a sentence is removed, the sentences that
depended on it do not break — they go quiet. They still parse, still sound
finished, and still read as true. But what they refer to is no longer in the
piece.

The near-miss to watch for: **a sentence that is still grammatical is not a
sentence that still refers to something.** Words like *remained*, *yet*, *still*,
*each*, *the rest*, *this*, *that assignment*, *those* and *it* all point
backwards. If what they point at was cut, the line floats.

Invented example of the shape:

```
1  Vivian Keller photographed the same stretch of coast for thirty years.
2  Yet the pier remained, propped up by nothing but habit.
```

Line 2 concludes something. Nothing before it has said the pier was failing, or
that anything was expected to collapse — so *remained* and *yet* refer to a
premise that is not in the draft. The line is true, well made, and unanchored.

## What to ask of the draft

Read it in order. For each line, ask **what does this refer to, and is that thing
in the piece, earlier than this line?** Not whether the line is true — whether
its referent has been established.

Pay attention to: backward-pointing words with no antecedent; contrasts (*yet*,
*but*, *still*) with nothing to contrast against; quantifiers (*each*, *half*,
*the rest*) over a set that was never introduced; and a line that answers an
objection the draft never raises.

Do not report a line merely because it assumes general world knowledge the
audience will have. The test is whether the draft itself established the thing
the line points back at.
