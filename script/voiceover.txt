A copper penny is worth one cent. The copper inside it is worth about two and a half.
Rutherford Chang's CENTS begins in that gap.
Over several years he sorted a hundred thousand pennies down to ten thousand pre-1982 coppers.
He photographed each one and catalogued its date and mintage.
Then he melted them into a single block.
Then he inscribed each destroyed penny onto a sat, the smallest divisible unit of Bitcoin.
The block itself became a full-block inscription, CENTS Block 839969, the master object of the project.
He released all ten thousand inscriptions at one sat each, about one hundredth of a cent.
Every Cent passes through five transformations of value.
In circulation, its value defined by the Treasury.
Removed from circulation, that assignment subverted.
Archivally photographed, valued for what the image holds.
Destroyed, its value made undefinable.
Inscribed on Bitcoin, continually redefined by decentralized consensus.
And it arrives as five natures at once.
A unit of currency. A quantity of copper. A melted element of a sculpture. A photograph. A Bitcoin inscription.
All of these things, and none of them.
Conceptual art broke from the object in the nineteen sixties, when commodification had hollowed it out.
A split opened between materiality and meaning.
Digitization widened it. Digital flows could not hold identity, singularity, provenance or ownership.
They lacked objecthood.
Blockchain was built to give them discrete boundaries and verifiable histories.
Blockchain is this era's objecthood.
CENTS holds that whole arc inside one gesture.
Its medium is not the penny, or the photograph, or the block.
Its medium is the gesture of its continual becoming.
A digital object is simultaneously itself, not itself, and an interpretation of itself.
Its meanings keep emerging, as participants co-create the intersubjective reality of its presence.
