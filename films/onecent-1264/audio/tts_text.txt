[thoughtfully] [calm] The story of the value discrepancy at the core of CENTS is told in the traits of Cent number twelve sixty-four. The starting point for Rutherford Chang, the fact that the copper in one penny was worth about three times as much as a penny, has roots and implications that are activated by the system that he created with CENTS. In July nineteen forty-four, at Bretton Woods, forty-four nations tied their currencies to the U.S. dollar, and the dollar to gold, at thirty-five dollars an ounce. Gold was the measure of money. Struck in nineteen forty-four, Cent number twelve sixty-four is the oldest Cent in the collection with a gold trait, by twelve years. On August fifteenth, nineteen seventy-one, the United States stopped exchanging dollars for gold. Within two years the Bretton Woods system had collapsed, and currencies floated against each other. U.S. currency became fiat: worth what it is trusted to be worth. Dislocating the global reserve currency from any material substance had wide-ranging effects, not only on wealth distribution and social equity, but on materiality itself. These effects continue to bear influence to this day, in economics as well as art. In the words of Adina Glickstein, in her article "Never Midcurve" for Spike Magazine: "Post-Bretton Woods as much as post-internet, hasn't contemporary art become, if not exactly a commercial product, the financial instrument par excellence?" It is in the strange tension between twentieth century conceptual art and twenty-first century post-internet art, between absence of an object and digital objecthood, between value discrepancy and speculation, that CENTS resides.
