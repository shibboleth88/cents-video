[thoughtfully] [calm] In twenty seventeen Rutherford Chang said: "I'm going to start hoarding pennies." The penny, he said, is "the lowest common denominator, it's like junk, it's like nothing." He had, he said, "no idea" what he would make of them. What he made, CENTS, is a work of its medium. Chang inscribed images of ten thousand copper cents on Bitcoin, and the work shows what happens to money when its record passes from a state to a public network that anyone can read and no one runs. Released at one satoshi each, the inscriptions moved each cent's value from a figure set by the state into an ongoing process, set and set again by the people who keep, read and exchange each Cent. It began with copper. A copper cent struck before nineteen eighty-three holds more than a cent's worth of copper. Hoarding them, he said, was like "buying one point seven cents of copper for one cent and it's still worth one cent." Chang's statement for the work begins from such "discrepancies in value." He took ten thousand copper cents out of circulation and photographed every coin. Coins made to be identical turned out to differ, each marked by its own wear, toning and color. He had the coins melted and cast into one block, CENTS Block Copper. In March twenty twenty-four he inscribed the image of each obverse, one image to one satoshi, the smallest unit of bitcoin, and in April the block's model, which fills a whole block of the chain: CENTS Block eight three nine nine six nine. His statement calls it "an immutable digital monument." The coin, its image, its inscription, the copper and the model: each is a cent in its own way. Collectors preferred the damaged to the clean. A council of collectors reviewed and voted on all ten thousand Cents three times, and agreed on nineteen traits. Chang's statement ends: "its value can continue to be redefined through this digitally scarce set of ten thousand and the block that encapsulates their transformation."
