[thoughtfully] [calm] Cent thirteen twenty-eight.
In the words of the art historian Rosalind Krauss, in her nineteen seventy-seven essay "Notes on the Index": "Almost everyone is agreed about seventies art. It is diversified, split, factionalized."
Beneath that variety, she saw one shared logic: the index.
In that art, the record of a physical presence replaces the conventions of style.
Indexes are signs with a physical link to what they stand for, like footprints, cast shadows, or photographs.
In her words: "They are the marks or traces of a particular cause, and that cause is the thing to which they refer, the object they signify."
The marks on a Cent are of this kind: traces of real events, of wear, of damage, of the slow work of air.
Cent thirteen twenty-eight was struck in nineteen seventy-nine, the year Rutherford Chang was born, with no mint mark, generally indicating the Philadelphia mint.
Its first trait is a fingerprint. The ridges run across Lincoln's neck and into the field beside him.
The Traits Atlas sets a strict bar: "if one hesitates to see a fingerprint, it isn't a Fingerprint."
Two hundred and twenty-four Cents carry it.
Its second trait is White Drips and Streaks: pale runs of residue, like this one falling from the rim.
Of the eighty-two Cents with this trait, seventy-nine are also Desert, a pale toning. This is one of three that are not: here the white runs over bright copper.
Only three Cents carry both traits, and this is the only one with no other.
Compare Cent sixteen-oh-five, also from nineteen seventy-nine and close to mint state.
An index points back to its cause. Here, the cause is a finger, and whose, no one knows.
Someone has dreamed of finding out. In the words of collector Martred: "I dream someday to be able to match one of the fingerprints found on a few Cents, with the real person owning that fingerprint, it would be a kind of cosmic reconciliation of sorts."
Cent thirteen twenty-eight, a great Cent.
