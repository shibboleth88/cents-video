[thoughtfully] [calm] Cent thirteen twenty-eight.
A penny struck in nineteen seventy-nine with no mint mark, generally indicating the Philadelphia mint.
Its first trait is a fingerprint. The ridges run across Lincoln's neck and into the field beside him.
The Traits Atlas sets a strict bar: "if one hesitates to see a fingerprint, it isn't a Fingerprint."
Two hundred and twenty-four Cents carry it.
Its second trait is White Drips and Streaks: pale runs of residue, like this one falling from the rim.
Of the eighty-two Cents with this trait, seventy-nine are also Desert, a pale toning. This is one of three that are not: here the white runs over bright copper.
Only three Cents carry both traits, and this is the only one with no other.
Compare Cent sixteen-oh-five, also from nineteen seventy-nine and close to mint state.
The mark as index.
In the words of the art historian Rosalind Krauss, in her nineteen seventy-seven essay "Notes on the Index": "They are the marks or traces of a particular cause, and that cause is the thing to which they refer, the object they signify."
This mark points to a finger. Whose, no one knows. In the words of collector Martred: "I dream someday to be able to match one of the fingerprints found on a few Cents, with the real person owning that fingerprint, it would be a kind of cosmic reconciliation of sorts."
Transformations of value.
In circulation, the Treasury set this penny's value at one cent, fingerprint or not. Rutherford Chang photographed each of the ten thousand pennies, then melted them all into one copper block. The fingerprint survives only as an image, inscribed on one satoshi. As an image, it is valued for what the picture shows: a year, a touch, a run of white.
Cent thirteen twenty-eight, a great Cent.
