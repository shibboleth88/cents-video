A copper cent, struck in 1972, green with verdigris, its date legible through the corrosion.
One of ten thousand that Rutherford Chang took out of circulation.
He released them on Bitcoin at one satoshi each. By October 2026 the ones listed for sale were asking around three hundred and sixty thousand.
To the Treasury a penny is worth one cent, whatever year it was struck, and always will be.
Those struck before 1983 are copper, and in March 2024 that copper was worth about three cents.
For more than a century it has circulated, considered a worthless nuisance.
Three valuations of one small object, and no two of them agree.
Chang spent twenty years collecting ordinary things until time made them singular, among them three and a half thousand copies of one Beatles record, no two worn alike.
Shown for two decades — the New Museum in New York, the Akademie der Künste in Berlin, Kunsthal Rotterdam.
Recognising those discrepancies in value, he removed ten thousand copper cents from circulation and photographed every one.
Circulation made the marks: verdigris, abrasion, a fingerprint, the way each one left.
No algorithm assigned any of it. The names came afterwards, from the collectors, over two years of voting.
More than half the set carries no mark at all, which is what makes the rest mean anything.
Then he destroyed them, smelted into one block of thirty-one kilograms.
Each photograph he inscribed on a satoshi, the smallest unit of Bitcoin.
The block he had scanned, and inscribed as the whole of Bitcoin block 839969.
Chang died in February 2025, at forty-five, on the day the President ordered the Treasury to stop making pennies. The last was struck that November.
The set is closed. It stops at 1982, because in 1983 the penny stopped being copper.
Fifteen years are missing, and only one of those absences means anything: in 1943 the copper went to the war, and the cent was struck in steel.
A set with edges nobody chose.
The cent from 1972 no longer exists. Its image does, and what that is worth goes on being decided.
