Cent #8820. A penny struck in 1972 with no mint mark, generally indicating the Philadelphia mint.
Two patinas share its face.
Significant Red: in the Traits Atlas, red that is "a major visual actor", strong across several zones.
183 Cents carry it.
And Notable Verdigris: "localised but clearly visible patches or films". On Cent #8820 the green gathers in the lettering and along the rim, with grey-green patches on Lincoln's face like daubs of paint laid by time.
29 Cents carry exactly this pair, and nothing else.
Both are oxidation of copper. In the Atlas, red "marks a transitional oxidation phase between bright copper and darker patinas".
Beside Cent #8653, struck the same year and close to mint state, you can see how far Cent #8820 had moved from bright copper. As money, the two were worth the same: one cent.
Permanence and decay.
Walter Benjamin, in his 1936 essay "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction", wrote that the history of a work of art includes "the changes which it may have suffered in physical condition over the years as well as the various changes in its ownership."
A penny is mechanically reproduced from the start, one of billions. Yet time gave Cent #8820 a history of its own.
But Benjamin also wrote: "The presence of the original is the prerequisite to the concept of authenticity." The penny itself is gone.
Through Benjamin's lens, CENTS keeps both halves of that history: the photograph keeps the changes in physical condition, and Bitcoin's ledger records each transfer of the satoshi that carries its image.
Cent #8820, a great Cent.
