As currency, a penny is worth one cent, whatever has happened to it.
In CENTS, what happened to it is what tells one Cent from another.
Rutherford Chang removed ten thousand copper cents from circulation and documented each one.
The names for their marks came afterwards, from the collectors themselves, over two years of voting.
These are the traits they named.
The four base patinas come in three degrees.
Notable: clearly visible, but local.
Significant: a major presence on the face.
Full: the patina defines the coin.
Verdigris is green to teal, pooling in the low relief and along the lettering. Fifteen Cents are full verdigris.
Obsidian is brown to black, pushing the copper into shadow. Thirty-eight are full.
Desert is white, sand and ochre, dry and matte, as if baked by the sun. Twenty-one are full.
Red runs from scarlet to russet, a stage between bright copper and the darker patinas. Seventy-one are full.
Gold is honey and amber, the copper suspended between metal and light. Only nine are full.
Orange is tangerine and amber, kept for clear standouts only.
Iridescent is copper under a thin film that splits the light into gold, violet and blue. There are twenty.
Corroded goes deeper than color: crusts and pits where the metal itself has been eaten.
Some marks were made at the mint.
Lustrous: the original shine survives, Lincoln bright against a matte field.
Striated: parallel lines across the face, left by metal flowing under the force of the strike.
Some came from wear.
Abraded: copper physically missing, in gouges and chunks, notable or heavy.
Faceless: Lincoln's features lost, to wear, corrosion or residue, until the portrait reads as erased.
Some marks were added from outside.
Fingerprint: the ridges of a human touch, held in the copper. If you hesitate to see a fingerprint, it isn't one.
White drips and streaks: pale runs of residue, often across dark obsidian.
Contaminated: foreign matter on the coin, sorted into twelve types by how it looks and behaves.
The names are field names for what can be seen, not results of a lab test.
Glue: hardened amber films. Glue was approved only after a Swiss woodworkers' forum studied the photographs.
Obscure adhesive: thicker, cloudier deposits of unclear origin.
Scotch tape: a rectangle where tape once covered the metal. Seven Cents carry it.
Paint: a colored layer added after minting.
Dark grime: dirt and oil worked into the surface.
Sea salt: crystal blooms, bluish to white.
Speckles: small particles spattered across the face.
Unknown liquid: dried pools with tide-line edges.
Alien secretion: translucent greenish films, named for how they look.
3D-black: raised black deposits, thick as tar. There are thirty-five.
Blood: reddish-brown splashes that read like blood, though the Council does not claim they are. Seventeen.
And special oddities: forty-four that fit no other type.
Some marks became pictures.
Ghost: a full patina that separates Lincoln from the field, so the portrait floats like an apparition. Thirty-three.
Assassin: one round mark striking Lincoln's head, neck or body, like an imagined shot. Forty-two.
Vandalized: marks that read as deliberate, made by someone's hand, scratched, drilled or painted. Twenty-six.
And one trait is a date.
Wheat: every Cent struck before 1959, with wheat ears on the reverse. Two hundred and eight.
About half of all Cents carry no visual trait at all: a clean record of their year.
Every name here is a Council judgment as of September 2026, some reached after long debate and contested votes.
The marks were made by time and touch. What they mean is still being decided, by everyone who looks.
